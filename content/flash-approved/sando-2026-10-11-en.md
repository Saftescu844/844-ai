# SANDO: MIT's Approach to Safer Drone Flights in Unknown, Dynamic Environments

**Excerpt (EN):** MIT researchers describe SANDO, a system that replans drone routes around moving obstacles. Its mathematical safety guarantees depend on model assumptions, while the reported tests show promising results within evaluated conditions.

**Pilon:** Știri AI / AI News · **Tip:** Cercetare / Research · **Statut:** Propunere pentru revizie, nepublicată

MIT researchers have developed SANDO, a trajectory-planning system designed to provide formal collision-avoidance guarantees for uncrewed aerial vehicles under the assumptions of its mathematical model. The system addresses environments that are both unmapped and changing, where a UAV cannot rely on a pre-existing map and must account for obstacles whose future movements are not precisely known. The research tackles a difficult gap in autonomous navigation: many existing planners can offer formal safety assurances in static settings or when obstacles are known beforehand, but dynamic and unfamiliar environments make those assurances much harder to establish. The work has been published in IEEE Transactions on Robotics.

The central idea is to build a safety corridor that changes over time rather than treating a safe route as fixed. SANDO uses data from the UAV’s onboard cameras and sensors to identify and monitor obstacles, then estimates the area each obstacle could reach over a given period. It does not claim to know exactly where an obstacle will move. Instead, it requires knowledge of a valid upper bound on obstacle speed and constructs a surrounding region representing the farthest distance the obstacle could travel in any direction during that interval. The planner then creates its connected three-dimensional corridor outside those regions. This approach is intended to keep the planned route separated from possible obstacle positions as time passes.

That distinction matters because an initially clear route can quickly become unsafe when objects move into it. The researchers say SANDO repeatedly updates the corridor and reformulates the UAV’s trajectory while the vehicle is in flight. Within the established safe corridor, it optimizes for a fast route toward the goal. A heat-map-based component also identifies areas with many obstacles and steers the UAV away from these higher-risk regions, helping it seek a more efficient path around them. The system’s mathematical safety claim depends on assumptions about obstacle velocities and estimation errors, as well as continued replanning; it is not an unconditional guarantee for every real-world flight.

The potential relevance is clearest in missions where maps may be unavailable and conditions can change quickly, including search-and-rescue operations in collapsed buildings, exploration of hidden mine tunnels, or flight through crowded delivery areas. The MIT News article also describes a wildfire scenario in which a UAV could gather information for rescuers while avoiding hazards such as falling branches. These examples illustrate intended applications; they do not establish that SANDO has been deployed in those operations. The reported evidence remains bounded by the evaluations described by the researchers. According to MIT News, SANDO reached goals faster than several state-of-the-art systems in simulations while avoiding collisions in the tested environments. The same report states that the system avoided dynamic obstacles in 12 real-UAV test flights using onboard computing and sensors.

SANDO’s approach relies on a valid upper bound for obstacle speed and assumptions about estimation error. It rapidly recalculates trajectories using its onboard computer to respond to sudden changes, without claiming to predict exactly where an obstacle will move. Instead, the method estimates where moving obstacles could possibly go and keeps the planned UAV trajectory outside those regions. Future work could make the system more computationally efficient and combine it with machine-learning models that allow users to give a robot instructions in plain language. For autonomous flight, SANDO combines formal safety analysis with frequent onboard replanning in complex, dynamic environments.
